Watch someone let go of something difficult and you'll often see the same thing: a long, slow sigh. The shoulders drop, the face softens, and for a moment the body seems to unclench. We sigh like this naturally, without being taught. The long exhale is simply a way of doing it on purpose, gently and rhythmically, whenever you need a little steadiness.

The idea couldn't be simpler: breathe out for a little longer than you breathe in. Perhaps in for four, out for six. No holds, no special counts to memorise, nothing that's hard to remember when your mind is busy. It's one of the most accessible breath practices there is, and you can do it anywhere.

Why a longer out-breath feels calming

Your breathing and your heart rhythm are closely linked. When you breathe in, your heart tends to speed up very slightly; when you breathe out, it tends to slow. This natural rhythm is one reason why slow breathing with a longer exhale is widely taught as a way to settle the body. It's associated with the "rest and digest" side of the nervous system, the side that helps us unwind.

There's also a simpler reason: a long exhale gives the mind a clear, gentle task. Counting the out-breath, or following it all the way to its natural pause, gives attention something soft to rest on. When we're anxious, our breathing often becomes quick and high in the chest. Lengthening the exhale gently invites the breath back down toward the belly, at its own pace.

You don't need to take a bigger breath in. You only need to let the breath out a little more slowly.

How to practise

  1. Get comfortable. Sit or stand with your shoulders relaxed. You can rest a hand on your belly to feel it move.
  2. Breathe in gently through your nose for a count of about four. Don't force it; this is a normal, comfortable breath.
  3. Breathe out slowly for a count of about six, through your nose or through softly pursed lips, as if you were blowing gently on a spoonful of soup.
  4. Let there be a tiny natural pause at the bottom of the breath, if one wants to happen. Don't hold; just let the next in-breath arrive when it's ready.
  5. Continue for a few minutes. Five or six rounds is a lovely start; ten rounds is about two minutes.
  6. Return to natural breathing and notice how your body feels.

If four and six don't suit you, adjust. Three in and five out works well for many people. The ratio matters more than the numbers: just make sure the exhale is comfortably longer than the inhale, and that nothing feels strained.

Variations to explore

The sigh

Take a normal breath in through the nose, then a second small top-up sip of air, and let it all go in a long, slow sigh through the mouth. Two or three of these can feel like setting down a heavy bag.

Humming exhale

Breathe in, then hum softly on the out-breath, letting the sound last as long as the breath does. The vibration gives your attention something lovely to rest on, and the humming naturally lengthens the exhale.

Counting down

On each exhale, silently count backward from ten to one, one number per breath. When you reach one, start again. If you lose count, that's fine; simply begin again at ten.

When to use it

  • Before a meeting, a difficult conversation, or an important call.
  • When you notice your shoulders creeping up toward your ears.
  • While waiting: in a queue, at a red light, as the kettle boils.
  • In bed, as part of your evening wind-down.
  • Alongside box breathing, when you'd like something even simpler.
Exercise

Three breaths, three times a day

A tiny daily practice for building the habit. Tick each step as you complete it; your progress is saved on this device.

  1. Find your ratio. Try 4 in / 6 out, then 3 in / 5 out, and choose whichever feels easiest.
  2. Morning: before you look at your phone, take three long exhales while still in bed.
  3. Midday: pause before lunch or a break and take three long exhales.
  4. Evening: take three long exhales as you switch off the lights.
  5. Try one variation: the sigh, the hum, or counting down from ten.
  6. Use it in a real moment of mild stress, and notice what changes, if anything.

A few gentle cautions

Breathing practices should always feel comfortable. If you feel dizzy, light-headed, or uneasy, stop and return to your natural breath. If you have a respiratory or heart condition, or are pregnant, check with your doctor before beginning any new breathing practice. And while the long exhale can be a helpful everyday tool, it isn't a substitute for professional care if you're living with ongoing anxiety. Reach out for support whenever you need it; that, too, is an act of kindness toward yourself.
