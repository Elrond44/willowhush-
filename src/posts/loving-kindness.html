Most of us have a voice in our heads that speaks to us more harshly than we would ever speak to a friend. It notices every mistake, rehearses every worry, and rarely says anything kind. Loving-kindness meditation is a quiet, deliberate way of practising a different voice: one that wishes you well, and then, gradually, wishes everyone else well too.

Known in the Pali language as metta, loving-kindness is one of the oldest contemplative practices, taught for well over two thousand years. Its method is beautifully simple. You silently repeat a few warm phrases, such as "may you be happy," and offer them, in turn, to yourself, to people you love, to people you barely know, to people you find difficult, and finally to all living beings.

What loving-kindness is (and isn't)

Loving-kindness isn't about forcing a feeling of love, pretending everything is fine, or excusing anyone's behaviour. It's closer to watering a seed. You plant an intention of goodwill, and you water it with repetition. Some days you'll feel a rush of warmth; other days you'll feel nothing at all, or even irritation. All of that is welcome. The phrases still count, because the practice is the intention, not the emotion.

Many people find that over time this gentle repetition changes the tone of their inner voice. Kindness becomes a little more familiar, a little more available when it's needed, whether that's in a tense meeting, a traffic jam, or a sleepless night.

You yourself, as much as anybody in the entire universe, deserve your love and affection.

The traditional phrases

There are many versions of the phrases. A simple, widely used set is:

  • May you be happy.
  • May you be healthy.
  • May you be safe.
  • May you live with ease.

Feel free to change them until they sound like you. "May you feel at home in yourself," "may you be gentle with yourself," or "may you know you are loved" are all lovely alternatives. The best phrases are short, sincere, and easy to remember with your eyes closed.

The five circles

The practice usually moves through five widening circles. Spend a minute or two with each, repeating the phrases slowly, perhaps one phrase per breath.

  1. A loved one. Many teachers suggest starting here, because it's often easiest. Picture someone who makes you smile without effort, a friend, a grandparent, even a pet, and offer them the phrases.
  2. Yourself. Now turn the phrases inward: "May I be happy. May I be healthy." If this feels awkward, you're in good company. Try picturing yourself as a small child, or simply say the words as kindly as you can.
  3. A neutral person. Someone you see but don't really know: a neighbour, a bus driver, a barista. This circle reminds us that strangers have full, complicated lives too.
  4. Someone difficult. Begin with a person who causes mild friction, not the most painful relationship in your life. You're not condoning anything; you're simply wishing that they, too, might be free from suffering. Skip this circle whenever it doesn't feel right.
  5. All beings. Finally, let your wishes spread outward without limit: everyone in your home, your street, your city, your country, the whole world. "May all beings be happy. May all beings live with ease."

When it feels hard

It's very common for loving-kindness to stir up resistance: a voice saying "this is silly," or "I don't deserve this," or a wave of sadness. These reactions aren't signs that you're doing it wrong. Often they're simply the places that most need kindness showing themselves. Breathe, soften your face, and keep the phrases gentle. If a circle feels like too much, return to the loved one, where the warmth comes more easily.

You might also find that your attention wanders far more than in breath meditation. That's normal. Each time you notice, just pick up the next phrase and carry on, without commentary.

Bringing it into the day

Loving-kindness doesn't have to stay on the cushion. Try silently offering a phrase to the people you pass on the street, the colleague who just interrupted you, or the person ahead of you in a slow queue. It takes a second, and it subtly changes the way you see the moment. You might also pair a short round with your morning meditation, setting a warm tone for the hours ahead.

Exercise

A ten-minute loving-kindness sit

Work through the circles one at a time and tick each as you complete it. Your ticks are saved on this device; print the list if you'd like it beside you.

  1. Settle in. Sit comfortably, rest a hand on your heart if you like, and take three soft breaths.
  2. Choose your phrases. Use the traditional four or write two or three of your own.
  3. Offer them to a loved one for about two minutes, picturing their face.
  4. Offer them to yourself for about two minutes, as kindly as you can manage.
  5. Offer them to a neutral person you saw this week.
  6. Offer them to someone mildly difficult, or skip this step today if you need to.
  7. Widen to all beings, letting the wish travel as far as you can imagine.
  8. Rest and notice. Sit for a final minute and notice how you feel, without judging it.

Be patient with this practice. Kindness, like a willow, grows slowly, bends often, and is far stronger than it looks.
