Sleep & Rest

A body scan for restless nights

By ElrondNovember 1, 20268 min read

Some nights, the body lies down long before the mind agrees to. The lights are off, the pillow is right, and yet something keeps humming: a jaw held a little too tight, shoulders still braced against the day, a list quietly reciting itself in the background. You can't argue yourself to sleep. But you can give your attention somewhere softer to rest, and the body is always right there, waiting to be noticed.

A body scan is one of the simplest ways to do that. You move your attention slowly through the body, from the crown of the head to the soles of the feet, noticing what is there without trying to fix it. It isn't a trick for forcing sleep. It's more like walking through a house at night, gently switching off the lights one room at a time.

Why the body is a good place to land

Thoughts are fast and slippery; sensations are slow and concrete. When you're replaying a conversation or rehearsing tomorrow, attention keeps leaping ahead. The body, by contrast, only ever exists in this moment. The weight of your heel on the mattress is happening now. The warmth of your hands is happening now. Each time you return to a sensation, you step out of the story and back into the room.

Many people also find that simply noticing tension invites it to loosen. You may discover your tongue pressed to the roof of your mouth, or your toes curled, or your brow drawn together. Often, the moment you see it, it begins to let go on its own. And if it doesn't, that's fine too. The practice is the noticing, not the outcome.

You don't have to relax. You only have to notice, one place at a time, and let each place be exactly as it is.

Before you begin

Lie on your back if that's comfortable, with your arms a little away from your sides and your legs uncrossed. If you sleep on your side, that works too; the scan is about attention, not posture. Let the bed take your weight. Take two or three slower breaths, letting each exhale be a little longer than the inhale, and then let the breath go back to doing what it does by itself.

A tip for the darkIf you'd like a gentle guide, the interactive scan below lights up each area of the body in turn, and can read the steps aloud so you can close your eyes. Set your screen to its dimmest setting, or use the five-minute guided body-scan video, then put the phone face-down and simply listen.

The scan, slowly

Move through each area at an unhurried pace, spending roughly half a minute to a minute in each place. There's no need to visualise anything special or to feel a particular way. If you notice nothing at all in an area, that's an observation too: "nothing much here." Then move on.

  1. Crown and forehead. Notice the top of your head, the skin of your forehead, the space between your eyebrows. Imagine it smoothing, like a sheet being shaken out and laid flat.
  2. Face and jaw. Soften around the eyes. Let the cheeks hang. Let the jaw drop a fraction so the teeth part, and let the tongue rest on the floor of the mouth.
  3. Neck and shoulders. Here is where many of us carry the day. On an out-breath, let the shoulders slide down a centimetre, away from the ears.
  4. Arms and hands. Feel the weight of the upper arms, the elbows, the forearms. Notice the palms and each fingertip, any warmth, tingling, or heaviness.
  5. Chest and belly. Feel the gentle rise and fall. Let the belly be soft; there's no need to hold it in here.
  6. Hips and lower back. Notice exactly where your body meets the mattress. Let that contact widen, as if you were sinking a little deeper with every breath.
  7. Legs and feet. Travel slowly down the thighs, the knees, the shins and calves, and finally the heels, soles, and toes. Imagine any leftover tension draining out through the soles of your feet.
  8. The whole body. Finally, widen your attention to include the whole body at once, breathing and resting. Stay here as long as you like.

When the mind wanders (and it will)

At some point you'll realise you've been planning breakfast or replaying an email for the last few minutes. This isn't failure; it's the most ordinary thing in the world. The moment you notice you've wandered is a moment of waking up. Simply note "thinking," and return to the last place in the body you remember. You might return a hundred times. Each return is the practice.

If you fall asleep partway through, wonderful. You don't need to finish the scan; there's no prize for reaching the toes. And if you reach the end still wide awake, you can begin again from the top, or simply rest in the feeling of the whole body breathing. Resting quietly with your eyes closed is still rest.

Making it a nightly habit

Like most practices, the body scan becomes more familiar, and often more effective, with repetition. Over time, the first few stops begin to act as a cue: your body recognises the pattern and starts to soften before you even reach the shoulders. Try it for a week, at roughly the same point in your bedtime routine, and notice what changes. Pair it with our 20-minute evening wind-down for an even gentler landing.

Exercise

Tonight's body-scan checklist

Tick each step as you go, or print it and keep it by your bed. Your ticks are remembered on this device, so you can pick up where you left off.

  1. Prepare the room. Dim the lights, silence notifications, and put your phone out of reach (or face-down once the guidance starts).
  2. Get comfortable. Lie on your back or side, uncross your legs, and let the bed take your weight.
  3. Three longer exhales. Breathe in naturally; breathe out slowly, as if fogging a window.
  4. Scan the head and face. Smooth the forehead, soften the eyes, unclench the jaw.
  5. Scan the shoulders, arms and hands. Let the shoulders drop; feel the weight of each arm.
  6. Scan the chest, belly and hips. Notice the breath moving; let the belly be soft and the hips heavy.
  7. Scan the legs and feet. Travel down slowly and imagine tension draining out through the soles.
  8. Rest in the whole body. Feel everything breathing at once. If you're still awake, begin again kindly.

A gentle note

The body scan is a relaxation and attention practice, not a treatment. If you live with ongoing sleeplessness, pain, or distress, please speak with a doctor or a qualified professional; they can help you find support that fits. And if any area of the body feels uncomfortable to focus on, it's always fine to skip it, or to rest your attention on your breath or your hands instead. Sleep well, friend.
